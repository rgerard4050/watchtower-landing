THE REAL JOURNEY

What actually happens to your material

Picture a truck backing up and dumping a mixed load of metal onto a facility floor. Here's the actual path it takes from there.

A magnet on a claw grabs the bulk of it — anything that's mostly iron or steel, which the trade calls "ferrous" material, the stuff a magnet sticks to — and drops it onto a moving conveyor belt.

From there it gets thrashed: tumbled and knocked around hard enough to shake loose anything that isn't actually bonded metal, like dirt or wood that rode along for the ride.

More magnets pull the true ferrous material out as it moves, narrowing the pile down step by step. What's left goes into a shredder, which tears everything down into fist-sized chunks and smaller.

From there, screens that vibrate like giant sieves sort the shredded pieces by size — bigger chunks one way, fine material another. It gets shredded a second time, then passes more magnets, then cyclones: spinning air chambers that separate material by weight, since light and heavy pieces fly apart differently inside a fast-moving column of air.

Then optical and AI sorting takes over. Cameras and sensors identify what each piece is actually made of — aluminum, copper, brass, stainless — and precisely timed blasts of air punch each type off the belt into its own bin, piece by piece, in a fraction of a second.

The finest material comes off as its own dust-sized grade. Last, a water table pulls out concentrate: a vibrating, slightly tilted table with water running over it, sorting the finest, heaviest particles by weight, particle size, and vibration alone. Crews are filling five-gallon buckets with concentrate carrying real precious metal — before any of it has ever been melted down.

Every one of those steps exists for one reason: to un-mix material that got mixed together at your curb. That's millions of dollars of machinery doing a job that two hands could have done in about ten minutes with a bucket and a little patience.

Whoever does that un-mixing work is the one who captures that value. Today, that's almost never the homeowner — it's whoever downstream ends up sorting what the homeowner didn't. That's the real, honest reason separating pays. Not a slogan. Just: the sorting work is worth real money, and right now you're usually not the one getting paid for doing it.

THE BIG PICTURE

What a circular economy is, and why it matters here

A circular economy just means material gets used, recovered, and used again — instead of being dug up or manufactured new every single time and used only once. Metal, glass, and many plastics can cycle through that loop again and again without losing much quality.

Here's why that matters locally and not just as a slogan: every pound of material pulled back into that loop from an Ocala driveway is a pound that didn't need to be mined, drilled, or manufactured from scratch somewhere else. That's real, physical value sitting in a garage or a bin right now — not an abstraction.

Watchtower exists to make capturing that value easy for a regular person, not just for industrial buyers with a loading dock and a scale.

SORTING GUIDE

Plastics: why the numbers matter

Flip most plastic items over and there's a small triangle with a number, 1 through 7, molded into it. That's the resin code, and it tells you what kind of plastic it actually is — which matters more than most people realize.

#1 (PET) — water and soda bottles — and #2 (HDPE) — milk jugs, detergent bottles — are the two most consistently wanted types, with a steady, established market for both.

#4 (LDPE, most bags and film), #5 (PP, yogurt tubs and bottle caps), and #3 (PVC) sit in a much spottier market. Sometimes there's a buyer nearby, sometimes there isn't, and it shifts with local demand.

#6 (PS, foam and rigid polystyrene) and #7 ("other," including mixed or layered plastics) are usually not worth sorting out separately at all.

Here's the part that catches people off guard: these resins can't just be melted down together. Each one melts at a different temperature and doesn't bond with the others when mixed, so a batch that's supposed to be pure #1 but has a few stray #5 caps in it isn't just "a little less pure" — it can fail entirely. That's why sorting by number is worth the extra few seconds.

THE HIDDEN COST

Contamination: why a little ruins a lot

A little contamination doesn't just shave a little value off a load. Often it drags the whole load down to the value of the worst thing in it. Some real examples of how that plays out:

Cardboard: a clean, dry, flattened bale is a straightforward, reliably sellable commodity. The same bale with food grease soaked through it, or a layer of wet material against the bottom, gets rejected outright by some buyers and steeply discounted by the rest. One greasy pizza box can drag down the grade of everything baled around it.

Aluminum cans: a clean load of aluminum cans is one of the best-paying common materials a household has. Mix in a handful of steel cans and a buyer will either pay the whole load at the lower steel rate, or make you sort it out on the spot before they'll take it. Contamination doesn't average the value down — it can flatten the whole load to the cheapest thing in it.

Copper wire: wire with the insulation still on sells for noticeably less than bare, stripped copper, because the buyer now has to do (or pay someone to do) the stripping work you could have done yourself. That cost gets deducted straight out of what they'll pay you.

THE RELIABLE STUFF

The unglamorous stuff that pays the bills

Nobody brags about saving up cardboard. But a few unglamorous materials are the ones that reliably pay, load after load — not the jackpot finds, the steady ones.

Cardboard doesn't feel like "scrap," but it's high volume and consistently wanted. Flatten it, keep it dry, and it moves.

Aluminum cans are the best per-pound return of any common household material that almost everyone already has sitting around. Used beverage cans are such a standard category in the trade that they're practically their own market.

Lead-acid batteries — car batteries and similar — are heavy, and genuinely hazardous if cracked open, which is exactly why they're tightly regulated and reliably bought back. Never crush or drain one yourself. Handled right, an old battery is one of the more dependable single-item paydays sitting in a garage.

MOVING MATERIAL

How to bundle, sell, and auction material

Bundling: loose material is a hassle for a buyer to handle. Flattening cardboard, bagging cans, coiling wire, and grouping like materials with like turns a pile into a load a buyer can move fast — and buyers consistently pay better for a load that's fast and easy to handle.

Selling: most everyday material moves through a straightforward exchange — here's what you have, here's what a buyer will give for it. That's the default, and it's the right approach for routine loads.

Auctioning: unusual, larger, or higher-value finds — a full estate clear-out, a pallet of mixed electronics, a specialty metal — often do better going to the highest bidder instead of the first offer. Different buyers value different mixes of material differently, so letting more than one buyer compete for an unusual load is how you find out what it's actually worth, instead of guessing.

SIDE INCOME

Buying a crinkler: packing filler as a side income

A crinkler is a small machine that feeds cardboard through rollers and cuts it into springy, crinkled strips — instant packing filler, the paper alternative to bubble wrap or foam peanuts.

Instead of sending clean cardboard out as flat scrap, some people run it through a crinkler first and sell the filler directly to people who ship things: small online sellers, local businesses, anyone packing boxes who'd rather not keep buying plastic filler.

It's a real, low-barrier side income. The machine is the main upfront cost, the raw material is cardboard sourced the same way you'd source it for scrap, and the buyers are local small shippers instead of a scrap yard.

What a smart node could become

A future shared node could combine a clear material label, QR or NFC identity, weight or fill sensing, and a known pickup location. The purpose is simple: make clean material easier to identify and move.

No smart-node hardware or community deployment is live today. These are design goals that require a willing host, safe placement, and verified operations.
